Repeater Run

B. Kaganovitch and Y. Ivanov, University of Toronto. Qollab x IonQ Hackathon, October 2026.

A quantum network runs on entangled pairs shared between distant nodes, used for teleportation, distributed computation and key distribution. Fibre loss grows exponentially with distance and a quantum state cannot be amplified, so a quantum repeater builds long-distance entanglement from short segments. Entanglement swapping, a Bell measurement at an intermediate node, joins two pairs into one pair spanning both segments and compounds their noise. Entanglement purification consumes two identically prepared pairs and can keep one with higher fidelity when their input fidelity exceeds 0.5. On this page you build a repeater chain over the 540 km route from Toronto to Montreal, and it is scored by the fidelity and rate of the delivered pairs. Each operation also runs as an 8-qubit circuit on the selected backend.

Usage

  1. Select the number of segments and the fidelity F0 of an elementary pair.
  2. Swap at a node joins the two adjacent links (Eq. 2). Purify consumes a second copy of a link (Eq. 3). Distilling entanglement from identical Werner inputs requires fidelity above 0.5; below that, undo and purify earlier.
  3. Once one link spans the route, the result row gives its fidelity, pair rate and secret key rate; Measure QBER samples its error rate on the backend.

Introduction

Teleportation, distributed quantum computation and entanglement-based key distribution (Bennett, Brassard and Mermin 1992) all consume entangled pairs shared between distant nodes. In fibre, the transmittance falls exponentially with distance and the no-cloning theorem (Wootters and Zurek 1982) forbids amplification, so the rate of any direct scheme decays with the transmittance (Pirandola et al. 2017). The quantum repeater (Briegel et al. 1998) avoids this limit: entanglement is generated over short segments, extended by swapping (Żukowski et al. 1993) and restored by purification (Bennett et al. 1996), at a cost polynomial in distance. A repeater design fixes the number of segments and the order of swaps and purifications, trading pair rate against fidelity, both paid for in elementary pairs.

Theory

1. Loss and heralding

Fibre attenuates 0.2 dB/km at 1550 nm; over the 540 km route one photon in 6 x 1010 arrives. A segment of length L delivers a heralded elementary pair per attempt with probability

η = 10−0.02 L/km,  τ = L/(cf η)(1)

where τ is the mean time per pair when each attempt awaits confirmation, with cf = 2 x 105 km/s. For one segment spanning the route, τ = 5.4 years; for four segments of 135 km, 338 ms.

2. Werner pairs and swapping

Each link is a Werner state (Werner 1989), ρ = F|Φ+⟩⟨Φ+| + (1 − F)/3 (1 − |Φ+⟩⟨Φ+|), with Werner parameter w = (4F − 1)/3. A Bell measurement at the shared node and a Pauli correction at the far end join two links; the Werner parameters multiply,

w′ = w1w2(2)

For n identical elementary segments, w = w0n and F = (1 + 3w)/4: it is F − 1/4 that decays geometrically.

3. Purification

In the recurrence protocol of Bennett et al. (1996), both nodes apply a CNOT from the kept pair to the sacrificial pair and measure the latter in Z. The kept pair is retained if the outcomes agree. With ai = (1 − Fi)/3,

F′ = (F1F2 + a1a2)/p,  p = (F1+a1)(F2+a2) + 4a1a2(3)

A failed check discards both pairs, so the cost of the kept link is 2/p times that of its inputs. The output is twirled back to Werner form. Without the twirl, residual errors accumulate in Φ−, to which the Z-parity check is blind: a second round from F = 0.95 then lowers F from 0.965 to 0.934, whereas with the twirl it rises to 0.976. The protocol of Deutsch et al. (1996) reaches 0.998.

4. Rates

Segments operate in parallel; swapping leaves each segment's demand unchanged and purification multiplies it by 2/p. With M the largest expected demand over the segments, we use T = Mτ as an optimistic resource-time estimate. It omits synchronization between probabilistic links and assumes ideal buffering; 1/T is an idealized throughput estimate, not the expected first-pair arrival rate. Fidelity and rate are combined by the secret key rate of entanglement-based BB84. Measuring a Werner pair in Z or X gives

QBER = 2(1 − F)/3(4)

and the asymptotic secret fraction (Shor and Preskill 2000), with h the binary entropy,

r = max{0, 1 − 2h(QBER)}(5)

whose positive branch vanishes at approximately QBER = 11%, i.e. F = 0.835. The game uses a conservative 11% threshold and assumes asymptotically efficient basis sifting. The secret key rate is

R = r/T,  T = Mτ(6)

No scheme without repeaters exceeds the Pirandola-Laurenza-Ottaviani-Banchi (PLOB) bound per channel use (Pirandola et al. 2017),

RPLOB = −log2(1 − ηtot) ≈ 1.44 ηtot(7)

which is 0.023 bits/s for 540 km at 109 channel uses per second.

5. Circuits

Each operation runs on 8 qubits: two Bell pairs and two ancillas per pair. The ancillas, prepared by RY rotations, control X and Z on one qubit of the pair, which after tracing out leaves exactly the Werner state. The Bell measurement of a swap is deferred, with the corrections as controlled gates, since the built-in simulator does not support classically conditioned gates and IonQ backends do not support mid-circuit measurement. The QBER circuit measures one copy in Z and one in X. The protocol consists of Clifford operations on Pauli noise and is classically simulable; the circuits verify Eqs. (2)-(4) and claim no speedup.

6. Worked example

Four segments, F0 = 0.95, so w0 = 0.933 and τ = 338 ms.

Swaps only. Three swaps give w = w04 = 0.759, F = 0.819 and QBER = 12.1%: r = 0. A pair arrives every 338 ms but carries no secret key.

Purify, then swap. One purification per segment gives F = 0.965 with p = 0.936, so M = 2/p = 2.14. Swapping gives w = 0.9534 = 0.826, F = 0.869, QBER = 8.7% and r = 0.147. With T = 2.14 x 338 ms = 0.72 s, R = 0.20 bits/s, 8.9 times the illustrative pure-loss repeaterless bound at one billion channel uses per second, for 8.55 elementary pairs per delivered pair. R comes from the idealized resource model and is not a measured communication rate.

Two segments. Without purification F = 0.903, but τ = 5.7 min, so R = 9 x 10−4 bits/s, below the PLOB bound.

7. Limitations

Elementary pairs start at F0, standing in for source and memory imperfection. Memories and local operations are ideal, classical communication is instantaneous and nodes are equally spaced. The rate model also omits stochastic synchronization and assumes sufficient buffering. The PLOB line refers to a single pure-loss channel at the stated channel-use rate. Each circuit checks one operation from pairs at the current fidelities. Rates are asymptotic; finite-key rates are lower. The search for best known strategies allows up to six purification rounds per nesting level.
