Quantum supersampling of supermassive black holes

B. Kaganovitch and Y. Ivanov, University of Toronto. Qollab x IonQ Hackathon, October 2026.

A spinning, charged black hole is rendered pixel by pixel, each pixel the mean of 1024 traced light rays, and that mean is estimated at a fixed query budget either by averaging randomly drawn rays or by quantum amplitude estimation. The page shows where the quantum estimator wins, on the photon ring, the disk edges and the stars, and where it does not: on smooth regions, and on the stored rays themselves, which a classical computer can simply add up.

Usage

  1. Play steps through the nine passes; both renders converge to the reference.
  2. Clicking a pixel runs its amplitude-estimation circuits on the selected backend (Fig. 3).
  3. Method sets the right plate to the hybrid or to MLAE on every pixel; Display shows the image, the log error map, the reference, or the advantage map (where amplitude estimation overtakes random sampling).

Introduction

Shadows, reflections and gravitational lensing in computer graphics, optics and astronomy are commonly computed by ray tracing or path tracing, which assign each pixel the average of many light paths and are the standard for physically based images. The mean over a pixel's rays is a Monte Carlo integral. If the ray integrand can be evaluated coherently as an oracle, quantum amplitude estimation (Brassard et al. 2002) estimates it to precision ε with O(1/ε) oracle calls, against O(1/ε2) independent samples (Montanaro 2015); applied to rendering, this is quantum supersampling (Johnston 2016). Here the rays are precomputed, so the oracle encodes a finite table of 1024 values per pixel, which a classical computer can also average exactly in 1024 reads. The comparison with independent (IID) sampling therefore models a continuous integrand, and the finite-table baseline is shown beside it. We examine its query efficiency and its output on Kerr-Newman black holes with parameters motivated by the supermassive black hole Sagittarius A* (Sgr A*).

Theory

1. Spacetime and rays

In Boyer-Lindquist coordinates and geometric units (G = c = M = 1), the Kerr-Newman metric depends on the spin a and charge Q through

Σ = r² + a² cos²θ,  Δ = r² − 2r + a² + Q²(1)

with outer horizon r+ = 1 + (1 − a² − Q²)1/2. Photons are uncharged and therefore follow null geodesics (ds² = 0) of the metric. The Hamiltonian separates as

2ΣH = Δ pr² + pθ² + (L/sinθ − a sinθ)² − (r² + a² − aL)²/Δ = 0(2)

with energy E = 1, axial angular momentum L and the Carter constant conserved. Rays are integrated backwards with fourth-order Runge-Kutta in Mino time τ, defined by dλ = Σ dτ with λ the affine parameter, from a zero-angular-momentum observer at r = 40 until they cross the horizon, escape to r = 1000 (star field), or cross the disk plane. The traced shadow boundary agrees with the analytic photon-orbit boundary to 7×10−4 M; the Carter constant drifts by less than 4×10−7.

2. Disk emission

We assume a geometrically thin, optically thin accretion disk between the innermost stable circular orbit and r = 10. Gas follows prograde circular orbits with angular velocity

Ω = (r − Q²)1/2 / (r² + a(r − Q²)1/2)(3)

A photon of angular momentum L emitted there is shifted in frequency by g = 1/(ut(1 − ΩL)). With x = rISCO/r, the emitted flux follows the thin-disk profile F ∝ x³(1 − x1/2) and the temperature T ∝ F1/4; the observed brightness is g⁴F and the colour is the blackbody colour of gT. Here g is referred to a static observer at infinity, whereas rays start at a camera at finite radius; for the Sgr A* camera and L = 0 the two conventions differ by a factor 1.026 in g (1.108 in g⁴), so the shading is infinity-referred. The temperature scale, lane texture, filmic tone map and bloom filter are artistic and identical for every image shown.

3. Pixel integral and Monte Carlo

A pixel's luminance is the mean of its S = 1024 ray values Ij ∈ [0, 1], μ = S−1 Σj Ij. Averaging N rays drawn at random gives an unbiased estimate μ̂ with root-mean-square error

εMC = σ N−1/2(4)

where σ² is the variance of the Ij (IID sampling, the model of a continuous integrand). Reading the stored values without replacement instead gives εfin = σ((S − n)/(n(S − 1)))1/2 after n = min(N, S) reads, which is zero from N = S. Smooth pixels have small σ and converge quickly; edges, the photon ring and stars have large σ.

4. Amplitude estimation

With n = log2 S index qubits and one ancilla, the circuit A applies Hadamard gates to the index register and a uniformly controlled rotation Ry(2 arcsin Ij1/2) to the ancilla, so that

A|0⟩ = (1 − μ)1/2 |ψ0⟩|0⟩ + μ1/2 |ψ1⟩|1⟩,  μ = sin²θ(5)

The Grover operator Q = A S0 A† Sχ acts as a rotation by 2θ in the plane spanned by |ψ0⟩|0⟩ and |ψ1⟩|1⟩, so after k iterations the ancilla is found in |1⟩ with probability

Pk(1) = sin²((2k+1)θ)(6)

Maximum-likelihood amplitude estimation (Suzuki et al. 2020) measures s = 32 shots at each k ∈ {0, 1, 2, 4, ..., 128}, records the hits hk, and maximizes

log ℒ(θ) = Σk [hk log Pk + (s − hk) log(1 − Pk)](7)

Counting each use of A or A† as one query, N = s Σk(2k+1), and the error approaches the Heisenberg scaling

εAE ∝ N−1(8)

For this amplitude encoding, ideal shot probabilities depend on μ, rather than separately on the ray variance σ². The crossover against IID sampling depends on μ, σ, the circuit schedule and estimator failures; roughly 10³ is an empirical scale for the selected high-variance pixels, not a universal threshold. A finite table can instead be enumerated exactly.

5. Hybrid rule and error maps

The hybrid spends the first 32 queries per pixel on Monte Carlo rays and computes ρ = σ̂32²/(μ̂32(1 − μ̂32)) from their sample mean μ̂32 and sample variance σ̂32². At budget N, a pixel with ρ > 300/N spends the remaining N − 32 queries on amplitude estimation: the schedule up to the next k, with as many shots at that k as fit (21 to 31). If the estimate differs from μ̂32 by more than four conservative pilot standard errors, (max(μ̂32(1 − μ̂32), 1/32)/32)1/2, the pixel keeps its Monte Carlo estimate, a guard against a wrong likelihood mode of Eq. (7). Other pixels continue Monte Carlo and reuse the 32 pilot rays. The threshold 300 is inherited from the first version and was not recalibrated on held-out data. For an interior μ and a resolved likelihood mode, the MLAE variance approaches μ(1 − μ)/F with Fisher information F = s Σk(2k+1)², so for each pixel the advantage map shows the first budget of the schedule at which

(μ(1 − μ)/F(N*))1/2 < σ/(N*)1/2(9)

This asymptotic estimate ignores likelihood failures; on high-variance pixels its median, N* = 2176, agrees with the simulated crossover. The error display shows log10|μ̂ − μ| per pixel on a fixed scale from 10−5 to 10−1, so passes and methods can be compared directly.

6. Limitations

The target is the mean of quantized, tone-mapped grayscale ray values, not a calibrated radiance prediction. Reference chroma is supplied classically to every displayed method. Rays and oracle tables are precomputed; gate synthesis, data loading and ray tracing are excluded from the query count. Full-image passes are computed from the exact noiseless output distribution with three random seeds; Fig. 3 runs the circuits live. The finite table of stored rays is averaged exactly in 1024 classical reads, so these results are not evidence of a practical quantum speedup. The classical baselines are independent and without-replacement sampling; stratified, jittered or adaptive classical sampling, which also improves on N−1/2 at edges, is not compared. No hardware noise is modelled; on current hardware the deepest inspector circuits (816 CX) would retain little signal. The real charge of Sgr A* is negligible (Zajacek et al. 2018), so the charged preset is hypothetical.
