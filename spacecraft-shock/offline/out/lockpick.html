Lockpick

B. Kaganovitch and Y. Ivanov, University of Toronto. Qollab x IonQ Hackathon, October 2026.

No measurement distinguishes two non-orthogonal states with certainty. The lowest attainable error probability is the Helstrom bound; the Ivanovic-Dieks-Peres measurement instead never errs but sometimes returns no answer. The same limit bounds what an eavesdropper learns from a single intercepted state in two-state key distribution. Here a lock holds a qubit in one of two known states. The reader chooses the measurement; each choice runs as a circuit on the selected backend and is compared with both limits.

Usage

  1. Select the overlap s of the two lock states and the a priori probability p of ψ+.
  2. Set the measurement axis β by dragging in Fig. 1 or with the slider; Fig. 2 shows the predicted probability of a correct result (Eq. 2).
  3. Measure runs the circuit for both lock states. Unambiguous switches to unambiguous discrimination (Eq. 4).

Introduction

Distinguishing two known quantum states is the elementary problem of quantum detection theory (Helstrom 1976). It sets the information available to an eavesdropper on two-state key distribution (Bennett 1992) and the error rate of receivers for weak coherent light. The two standard strategies (Barnett and Croke 2009) minimise the error probability, or exclude errors and accept inconclusive results (Ivanovic 1987; Dieks 1988; Peres 1988). Both are realised here as circuits on one qubit per lock state, with one ancilla per state for the unambiguous measurement.

Theory

1. Lock states

The lock holds, with a priori probabilities p and 1 − p,

|ψ±⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ ± sin(θ/2)|1⟩,  s = cos θ(1)

They lie at polar angles ±θ in the x-z plane of the Bloch sphere and are prepared by RY(±θ) on |0⟩.

2. Projective measurement

A projective measurement along the Bloch axis at angle β (RY(−β), then Z) with outcome + read as ψ+ gives a correct result with probability

P(β) = p[1 + cos(β − θ)]/2 + (1 − p)[1 − cos(β + θ)]/2(2)

β = 0 is a measurement in the computational basis. Guessing without measurement gives max(p, 1 − p).

3. Helstrom bound

Over all measurements, including positive operator-valued measures (POVMs), the probability of a correct result is at most (Helstrom 1976)

PH = ½[1 + (1 − 4p(1 − p)s2)1/2](3)

attained by the projective measurement onto the eigenvectors of pρ+ − (1 − p)ρ−, which for p = 1/2 is the x axis, β = 90 deg.

4. Unambiguous discrimination

The Ivanovic-Dieks-Peres (IDP) measurement is a three-outcome POVM, realised by a Neumark dilation with one ancilla: a rotation RY(2γ) of the ancilla, controlled on the system in |0⟩, with cos γ = tan(θ/2), maps the two states to orthogonal states |±⟩ when the ancilla reads 0 and to a common state when it reads 1. Then

Pcorr = 1 − s,  P? = s,  Perr = 0(4)

for either state. This is optimal for p = 1/2; for unequal priors the optimum differs (Jaeger and Shimony 1995) and is not implemented.

5. Estimate

Each run measures both states with n = 4000 shots and estimates

P̂ = p q̂+ + (1 − p)q̂−,  σ2 = [p2q̂+(1 − q̂+) + (1 − p)2q̂−(1 − q̂−)]/n(5)

where q̂± is the fraction of correct identifications of ψ±.

6. Example

For s = 0.5 (θ = 60 deg) and p = 1/2: a measurement in the computational basis identifies ψ+ with probability cos2(30 deg) = 0.750 but ψ− only with sin2(30 deg) = 0.250, so P(0) = 0.500 (Eq. 2), equal to guessing; the x axis gives PH = (1 + 0.866)/2 = 0.933 (Eq. 3); the IDP measurement gives Pcorr = P? = 0.500 and Perr = 0 (Eq. 4).

7. Limitations

States are pure, the states and a priori probabilities are known, and only one copy is measured. Circuits are noiseless on the built-in simulator. Two-state discrimination on one qubit is classically simulable; the page illustrates measurement limits and claims no speedup.
