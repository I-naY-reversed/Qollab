Quantum Simulation of Pyroshock

B. Kaganovitch and Y. Ivanov, University of Toronto. Qollab x IonQ Hackathon, October 2026.

Pyrotechnic separation devices subject a spacecraft to pyroshock, a high-frequency transient that propagates through the structure to sensitive equipment; launch providers bound both the shock environment at the payload interface and the fixed-base fundamental frequency of the payload. Babbush et al. (2023) showed that the dynamics of 2n coupled harmonic oscillators can be simulated in time polynomial in n by encoding velocities and spring extensions in the amplitudes of a quantum state, so that the energy fraction of each mass and spring is a measurement probability. Here the algorithm is applied to a lumped-parameter tree model of a 12U CubeSat (eight masses, four qubits) in which the battery, the avionics board and an optical instrument sit on their own mounts: the transient from separation is propagated through the structure, the acceleration of each component is obtained from the energy of its mount, and battery mounts and instrument isolators are compared against the launcher's 40 Hz requirement and the components' allowable accelerations.

Introduction

Pyroshock from separation nuts, bolt cutters and frangible joints is characterised by accelerations of hundreds to thousands of g at frequencies up to 10 kHz (the SpaceX guide permits up to 2850 g SRS at the payload interface, Table 4-5) and can damage electronics and optics. Launch providers specify the shock response spectrum (SRS) at the payload interface together with a minimum fixed-base natural frequency, and sensitive instruments are often mounted on isolators, which attenuate the transmitted shock but lower that frequency. Babbush et al. (2023) proved that simulating 2n coupled classical oscillators is BQP-complete and can be done in time polynomial in n when masses and spring constants are accessible by oracle, the initial state is efficiently preparable and the output is an aggregate quantity such as an energy. An impulse applied at one point of a structure and the energy reaching one component are of this form.

Theory

1. Lumped-parameter model

The CubeSat is a fixed-base axial tree of eight masses; each mass hangs from its parent by one spring. Main load path: separation ring 2 kg, lower frame 3 kg, power deck 4 kg, upper frame 5 kg, isolator plate 3 kg, optical instrument 3 kg; the battery (3 kg) is mounted on the power deck and the avionics board (1 kg) on the upper frame. The total is the 12U limit of 24.00 kg (CubeSat Design Specification Rev. 14.1, Table 1); the distribution is assumed. Structural springs have k = 3.5e7 N/m and the board mount 1.0e7 N/m. The design variables are the battery mount kbat (3.5e7, 3.5e6 or 1.0e6 N/m) and the instrument isolator kiso (3.5e7 to 2.0e5 N/m). The SpaceX Rideshare Payload User's Guide (v11, Sec. 4.1.1) requires f1 ≥ 40 Hz. The fixed base represents the launcher while the payload is still attached, and separation is idealised as an impulse: the separation ring starts at v0 = 1.56 m/s, the pseudo-velocity at the knee of the guide's maximum predicted shock environment, 1000 g at 1000 Hz (Table 4-5). Accelerations scale linearly with v0, so this choice sets which designs pass.

2. Quantum encoding

With displacement vector x, mass matrix M and stiffness matrix

Mx'' = −Kx,  K = Σe ke ue ueT(1)

where ue is the incidence vector of spring e, let y = M1/2x and let B have columns √ke M−1/2ue, so that BBT = M−1/2KM−1/2. Following Babbush et al.,

ψ = (y', iBTy),  ψ' = −iHψ,  H = −[[0, B], [BT, 0]](2)

and

|ψ|2 = Σj mj x'j2 + Σe ke(ue·x)2 = 2E(3)

The outcome probabilities of a computational-basis measurement are therefore the kinetic energy fractions of the masses and the potential energy fractions of the springs. A tree of N masses on a fixed base has N springs, so eight masses and eight springs occupy 16 basis states of four qubits; in general ⌈log2 2N⌉ qubits. The impulse corresponds to a single basis state.

3. Circuit

Basis state 2e is spring e and 2e+1 is mass e. H then couples spring e to mass e with weight √(ke/me) (even bonds) and to the parent mass p with weight √(ke/mp); when p = e − 1 these are chain bonds, otherwise branch bonds (upper frame and isolator plate). With H = HE + HC + HR, each a sum of commuting two-level terms, the second-order product formula (Strang 1968)

e−iHδt = E(δt/2) R(δt/2) C(δt) R(δt/2) E(δt/2) + O(δt3)(4)

is implemented as follows. Even bonds couple basis states that differ in qubit 0, so E(h) is a uniformly controlled RX rotation on q0 (Möttönen et al. 2004) with angle −2h√(ke/me) for control value e; C(h) is the same construction conjugated by the decrement |p⟩ → |p−1⟩. Each branch bond in R(h) is a two-level rotation: a CX ladder maps its pair of basis states to states that differ in one qubit, followed by a uniformly controlled RX with a single non-zero angle. The rotation angles are computed from the spring constants and masses alone; the propagator is not computed classically. With 128 steps over 3.7 ms (δt = 28.9 us) a step costs 96 CX gates and a circuit at most 12296.

4. Observable and failure criterion

Each component (battery, avionics board, instrument) is attached by a single spring, its mount, so its acceleration follows from the mount's potential energy Ec = pcE0, where pc is the probability of the mount's basis state and E0 = 2.44 J the release energy:

|ac| = √(2kcpcE0)/mc,  σ|a| = (|ac|/2) σp/pc(5)

with σp = √(pc(1−pc)/S) for S shots, so one circuit yields all three accelerations. As a toy failure criterion, a component fails when |ac| first exceeds an allowable acceleration: 50 g for the battery, 200 g for the avionics board and 20 g for the instrument (illustrative values, not qualification levels). The predicted time of failure is the first crossing of the exact |ac(t)|.

5. Exact solution

The classical baseline is modal superposition. The generalised eigenproblem Kφr = ωr2Mφr with mass-normalised modes, φrTMφs = δrs, gives for the impulse x(0) = 0, x'(0) = v0e0

x(t) = v0 Σr φr(φrTMe0) sin(ωrt)/ωr,  f1 = ω1/2π(6)

Equivalently, since H2 = diag(BBT, BTB), the eigenvalues of H are ±ωr, and with H = VΛV†

ψ(t) = V e−iΛt V† ψ(0)(7)

which yields every energy fraction at any time. Dense diagonalisation costs O(N3) operations and is instantaneous at N = 8; Eq. (7) agrees with direct numerical integration of Eq. (1) to 1e-8. All design conclusions on this page, including the predicted failure times, are drawn from it; the circuit estimates are shown beside it.

6. Complexity

The exponential speedup of Babbush et al. relies on a block encoding of H built from oracle access to masses and spring constants. The uniformly controlled rotations used here require 2n−1 CX gates on n qubits, so each step costs O(N) gates, and at eight masses the classical solution is instantaneous; no practical advantage is claimed. Block-encoding circuits for chains are given by Luangsirapornchai et al. (2025) and Dsouza et al. (2026). An energy fraction to additive precision ε requires O(1/ε2) shots, or O(1/ε) with amplitude estimation; the full trajectory is not accessible. A web and arXiv search (October 11, 2026) found no earlier application of the algorithm to spacecraft structures or shock.

7. Limitations

One-dimensional axial tree with eight lumped masses; mass distribution, mount stiffnesses and allowable accelerations assumed. Undamped, whereas the guide specifies Q = 10 to 50. The fixed base represents the launcher while the payload is still attached; separation is idealised as an impulse, and v0, taken from the shock specification, sets the absolute accelerations and therefore which designs pass; the highest natural frequency of the model is about 1.1 kHz, so only the low-frequency part of the pyroshock band is represented; base excitation (sine, random vibration) not modelled. Results cover the first transit, 3.7 ms after release; in the undamped model later reflections can exceed these peaks. Product-formula bias at 128 steps is below 1.6 standard deviations of the shot noise at every sampled time of every design.

Further directions

General structures

For a structure that is not a tree, or a tree whose branch bonds share masses, the couplings can be grouped into matchings by edge colouring, each matching a set of disjoint two-level rotations; the cost per step grows with the largest number of springs on one mass. A component attached by several springs requires the signed forces, which are not given by energy fractions alone.

Finite-element scale

Finite-element models of spacecraft have 105 to 106 degrees of freedom with sparse stiffness matrices. With oracle access to masses and spring constants, 106 degrees of freedom need about 21 to 23 qubits, and the quantities that decide survival, the energy transmitted into a component or the force in its mount, are aggregates of the kind for which Babbush et al. prove an exponential advantage. This requires the block encoding of refs. 2 and 3 and amplitude estimation for O(1/ε) sampling.

Damping and other structures

Structural damping (Q = 10 to 50) makes the dynamics non-unitary; it can be included by embedding the damped system in a larger unitary at the cost of ancilla qubits. The same equation, Mx'' = −Kx, governs buildings and bridges under seismic load, launch-vehicle stacks during stage separation and lattice vibrations in crystals, so the encoding and the circuit construction apply unchanged.
